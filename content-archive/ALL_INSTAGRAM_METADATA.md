## A1. Why Are There No Tigers in Sri Lanka? | READY
**Cover:** IG cover from video_assets (Sri Lanka)
India has wild tigers. Sri Lanka, under 50 km away, has none. Why?

It isn't distance. In the last Ice Age the sea was about 120 m lower, and India and Sri Lanka were joined by land for more than half of the last 500,000 years. A toe bone (about 16,500 years old) and a tooth found in Sri Lanka were tentatively identified as tiger in 2005, but that ID is still debated.

Best-supported explanation: the sea didn't keep tigers out. The island may not have kept them in.

What do you think happened to Sri Lanka's tigers? Save this for your next wildlife debate.

## A2. Tiger Island | NEEDS VO
**Cover:** Tiger_Island_cover.jpg
Tigers are strong swimmers. So why aren't they on every island?

Sundarbans tigers cross water channels every day. Malayan tigers once swam into Singapore. But one tiger arriving is not a population.

A population needs a male and a female, a big territory, enough prey and, in the long run, hundreds of tigers. That's why tigers walked into Bali over Ice Age land, but never crossed the deep Lombok Strait next door. Borneo, Palawan, maybe Sri Lanka: tigers came, and none are there today.

Swimming moves a tiger. Land moves a population.

Which island should have had tigers? Tell me below.

## A4. Dhole reel | NEEDS VO + build
**Cover:** to be made with the reel
Tiger and leopard get the attention. The dhole is the predator people forget.

Packs of 5 to 12, sometimes 30+. They talk in whistles in thick forest, take down sambar many times their size, and have been recorded chasing tigers off their kills.

Gone from over 75% of their historic range. Hunted as "vermin" in India until 1972. About 949 to 2,215 breeding adults left worldwide (2015 estimate).

Now new records: Assam (after about 30 years), Manipur's first ever, Ratapani's first. A comeback? Not yet.

## A13. Pilibhit: how a forest doubled its tigers | READY
25 tigers in 2014. 65 in 2018. How does a forest do that in four years?

Pilibhit lost tigers first: reports put the count at 40+ in 2010 and around 23 by 2013, with questions about poaching. After it became a tiger reserve in June 2014, NTCA credits patrol technology (M-STrIPES), habitat and grassland work, and strict action against poachers.

But the 65 includes 8 transit tigers, and tigers move across the Nepal border, so how much came from breeding and how much from movement can't be said exactly.

It won the first TX2 award in 2020. Now the test is the edge: a narrow forest beside sugarcane, where WTI recorded 26 big-cat conflict cases around Pilibhit in 2024.

## B5. Silhouette teaching | CHECK (bird species)
Most silhouettes fail before the shutter clicks.

The camera wants to expose for the bird. A silhouette needs the opposite: meter the bright sky beside the sun, dial in -1 to -3 EV, and let the bird go black.

Then three choices decide the frame. Wait for a clean profile. Keep each bird's shape apart from the next. Move until the sun sits exactly where you want it.

## B8. Laggar Falcon (8 slides) | READY
India's most common falcon eats lizards.

In one nest watched for a full season in 2019, spiny-tailed lizards made up 79% of what the chicks were fed. Elsewhere in Rajasthan, those lizards are a major part of its diet too.

The Laggar does not even build a nest. It takes over old ones, sometimes those of crows and Egyptian Vultures, and adds nothing.

It was once India's most common falcon. Today it is Near Threatened, with a suspected fall of 20 to 29% in about ten years. Pesticides, habitat loss and trappers who use it as bait for larger falcons are the main reasons.

Photographs by Hitesh Chawla (The Natural Angle).

## B11. Sanjay-Dubri: Mohan, the white tiger | READY
In 1951, a white tiger cub was found in the forests of what is now Sanjay-Dubri.

The Maharaja of Rewa captured him. His name was Mohan. Bred in captivity with a tigress named Radha, he became the ancestor of most white tigers in zoos worldwide. That coat is not magic. It is a recessive mutation in a single gene.

The story has a darker side. Captive white tigers are the product of generations of inbreeding, which researchers link to many of their health problems.

The forest behind that story is still alive today. Sanjay-Dubri is part of the Bandhavgarh-Sanjay-Guru Ghasidas-Palamau tiger landscape, and tigers still move through the corridors between them.

Photographs by Hitesh Chawla (The Natural Angle), shot in Sanjay-Dubri Tiger Reserve.

## B12. Mausi: the tigress who raised her sister's cubs (10-slide version) | READY
A tigress raised her dead sister's cubs.

In March 2022, tigress T18 was found injured beside a railway track in Sanjay-Dubri. She was rescued and treated but died within a day. She left four cubs, about nine months old. An adult tiger killed one.

About ten days later, the surviving three were found with T28, T18's sister and a resident tigress of the range. Reports say she took them in alongside her own younger cubs, gave the orphans priority in hunting training, and that by August 2022 the orphans hunted on their own and shared prey. Forest staff monitored the family, including from elephant-back, and waited about a year before going public.

Tigers are famously solitary. This family was not.

## B14. Why do these wings glow? (ibis backlight) | CHECK (species IDs)
Why do these wings glow?

The sun was behind the bird. Feathers are thin, so light passes through the edges and the whole wing lights up.

Backlight also hides colour. This black-headed ibis and the glossy ibis in slide 2 are easier to tell apart by head, bill and size than by shade.

Birds usually land into the wind, so I try to stand with the wind at my back and the sun in front. Rule of thumb only. Read the grass and water first.
